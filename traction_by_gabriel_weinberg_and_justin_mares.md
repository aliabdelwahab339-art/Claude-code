# Traction by Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares

## Core Idea
Most startups fail not because they build a bad product, but because they never figure out how to get customers. Traction — quantitative evidence of customer demand — trumps everything. There are 19 distinct traction channels, and the channel that works for you right now probably isn't the one you'd first assume. The Bullseye Framework gives you a systematic process for finding it.

## Key Frameworks

### What Traction Is
**Traction = quantitative evidence of customer demand.**

- Mobile app: growing download rate
- Subscription service: rising monthly revenue
- E-commerce: increasing transaction count

Naval Ravikant: "Traction is basically quantitative evidence of customer demand."

A startup's entire purpose is growth. Traction = growth. Without it, everything else is just a vanity project.

### The Two Big Startup Traction Mistakes
1. **Defaulting to familiar channels** — Founders test only the channels they've heard about or used before. This creates massive clustering in a few channels (social media, SEO) while ignoring 15+ others that might be far more effective.
2. **Not having a concrete traction goal** — Testing channels without knowing what "enough traction" looks like leads to quitting channels that could work, or continuing ones that can't move the needle.

### The 19 Traction Channels
Every business gets customers through one (or a few) of these channels:

1. **Targeting Blogs** — Getting featured in niche blogs that reach your ideal audience. Mint got 40,000 users pre-launch this way.
2. **Publicity** — Getting coverage in newspapers, magazines, TV, and mainstream media.
3. **Unconventional PR** — Publicity stunts, extreme customer service, anything that generates buzz through non-traditional means.
4. **Search Engine Marketing (SEM)** — Paid search ads on Google, Bing, etc. Immediate traffic, highly intent-based.
5. **Social and Display Ads** — Paid ads on Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, Reddit, and niche sites.
6. **Offline Ads** — TV, radio, billboards, infomercials, direct mail. Reach demographics hard to target online (seniors, commuters).
7. **Search Engine Optimization (SEO)** — Ranking organically for relevant keywords through content and link-building.
8. **Content Marketing** — Using blogs, guides, and educational content to attract and convert customers. OkCupid built an entire brand through data-driven blog posts.
9. **Email Marketing** — Building and monetizing a list. One of the highest-ROI channels for conversion and retention.
10. **Viral Marketing** — Building referral loops into the product so existing users bring new users. Dropbox, Airbnb.
11. **Engineering as Marketing** — Building free tools, calculators, and widgets that drive leads. HubSpot's Marketing Grader drove tens of thousands of customers.
12. **Business Development (BD)** — Strategic partnerships where both parties benefit. Half.com's AOL partnership led to eBay's $350M acquisition.
13. **Sales** — Direct, person-to-person sales processes. Essential for enterprise software, high-ticket B2B.
14. **Affiliate Programs** — Revenue-sharing programs where partners promote your product for a commission. HostGator, GoDaddy scaled this way.
15. **Existing Platforms** — Piggybacking on an existing platform's user base (App Store, Facebook, Twitter). Evernote's App Store focus drove millions of customers.
16. **Trade Shows** — Industry events where you demonstrate products and build relationships. SureStop sealed a partnership at a single trade show → 20,000+ sales.
17. **Offline Events** — Sponsoring or running conferences, meetups, workshops. MicroConf grew an entire community this way.
18. **Speaking Engagements** — Getting on stage at conferences and events to build authority and generate leads.
19. **Community Building** — Creating a community of users around your product (forums, groups, associations) that becomes self-sustaining.

### The Bullseye Framework (3 Steps to Finding Your Traction Channel)

**The core insight:** You can't know in advance which channel will work best. You have to test systematically.

**Step 1: Brainstorm (The Outer Ring)**
For all 19 channels, ask:
- How could I use this channel for my business?
- What would a test look like?
- What would success look like in this channel?

The goal is to generate plausible ideas for all 19, even the ones that seem unlikely.

**Step 2: Rank (The Middle Ring)**
Sort channels into three groups:
- **Column A (Most promising)** — Channels most likely to work at your current stage
- **Column B (Possible)** — Channels worth testing with smaller bets
- **Column C (Long shots)** — Channels unlikely to work right now

Focus your small-scale experiments on Column A (2-3 channels max).

**Step 3: Focus (The Inner Bullseye)**
Run cheap, fast tests on your Column A picks. You're trying to answer:
- How much does it cost to acquire a customer through this channel?
- How many customers can I get through this channel?
- Are the customers quality customers?

When one channel shows real promise — **go all in on it.** This is the Bullseye. One channel typically dominates at any given stage.

### Critical Path Framework
Define your current traction goal first, then identify only the activities required to reach it. Everything not on the critical path is a distraction.

**Steps:**
1. Define your current traction milestone (e.g., 1,000 paying customers)
2. Map the critical path — only steps that directly lead to that milestone
3. Ignore all other work until the milestone is hit

This prevents the common startup failure of building features no one needs while traction stagnates.

### Traction Thinking: The 50% Rule
Allocate 50% of your time to building/improving the product, and 50% to getting traction. Most founders do 90/10 (product-heavy), which is why most startups die.

### The Channel Lifecycle
No traction channel works forever. What works to get you from 0 to 1,000 users typically stops working at 100,000 users. Competitors copy your tactics. Platform algorithms change. Audiences saturate. You must continuously run Bullseye experiments at each stage to find the next channel.

DuckDuckGo's traction channel evolution:
- 100 → 1,000 users: SEO (ranking for "new search engine")
- 1K → 100K: Content marketing
- 100K → 1M: Social and display ads, publicity
- 1M → 10M: Business development

### The Most Underutilized Insight
The most underutilized traction channels in any industry are often the most promising. When every competitor is fighting over Facebook ads and SEO, the entrepreneur going to trade shows, building engineering tools, or doing BD partnerships faces far less competition.

## Actionable Takeaways
- Define your current traction goal before deciding which channel to test
- Brainstorm plausible tests for all 19 channels — including the ones that seem irrelevant
- The channel you'd assume works for your business may not be the best one — test first
- When a channel shows clear promise, go all in — don't spread thin across 5 channels
- Allocate 50% of your time to getting traction — not just building product
- The most underutilized channel in your industry is likely your biggest opportunity
- Every channel that works will eventually stop working — keep running Bullseye experiments at each growth stage
- "Traction is the best way to improve your chances of startup success. Traction trumps everything."
- If you charge for your product, traction = customers buying. If it's free, traction = growing user base.
